Part 1 · foundations

Definitions and drivers

Underpinning. Telemedicine is clinical care at a distance through telecommunications. Telehealth is broader, adding non-clinical services such as education and remote monitoring. Telepharmacy is the provision of pharmacist care at a distance, covering drug review and monitoring, dispensing, compounding verification, medication therapy management, counselling and clinical consultation. Digital health is the widest term and often reaches the patient directly through applications and portals. The drivers are access, workforce and continuity, and a service justified by access must be judged by whether it widens access.

Worked example. A district hospital with no overnight pharmacist proposes that a regional hub reviews its overnight orders remotely. That is telepharmacy, specifically remote order review, within telehealth. If the hospital also launched a patient application for medicine reminders, that part would be digital health reaching the patient directly, and would raise questions of literacy and equity that remote order review does not.

Explanation. The ASHP definition: a pharmacist uses telecommunications to oversee aspects of pharmacy operations or provide patient-care services at a distance.

Explanation. Digital health covers mobile applications, portals, monitoring devices and their data, extending care to the patient directly.

Explanation. A driver is also a criterion. If access is the justification, reach to the underserved is the measure.

Part 2 · models and applications

How telepharmacy is delivered

Underpinning. Remote order review and entry has a central pharmacist verify orders for remote sites and supervise technicians there. Remote dispensing supplies a medicine without a pharmacist physically present, under remote supervision. The intrasystem model serves facilities within one organisation that already share a network and pharmacy system; the intersystem model serves an external facility that may lack a pharmacy system and need a pharmacy module, with heavier contractual and governance arrangements. Applications span order review and dispensing, sterile-compounding verification with cameras and in-process controls, counselling and monitoring, clinical services such as medication therapy management and anticoagulation, and workload balancing across a network.

Worked example. Two hospitals in one directorate share a pharmacy system; adding remote order review needs little more than secure communication, an intrasystem arrangement. A private clinic outside the directorate with no pharmacy system would be an intersystem arrangement, requiring a pharmacy module, a service agreement and separate governance.

Explanation. ASHP describes remote checking using cameras with in-process verification steps, which also reduce staff exposure to hazardous medicines.

Part 3 · infrastructure, security and regulation

What a safe remote service requires

Underpinning. Technology must be fit for the task: a camera too poor to read a label makes a remote check unsafe. Security is a condition: the remote site accesses what it needs while the central site's wider records remain closed to it, access is restricted to staff processing orders, communication is encrypted and sessions managed, and the remote site is closed when the central pharmacy is closed. Regulation must be reviewed, not assumed: who may provide the service, which medicines are excluded or require face-to-face assessment, how consent is obtained, how data is protected and whose rules apply across jurisdictions. In Jordan, the national framework on excluded medicines, approvals, consent and data protection applies. Where the act of supply is itself the clinical decision, use a hybrid design with a clinician authorising release.

Worked example. A remote site leaves its telepharmacy workstation logged in overnight so that nurses can look up orders. This breaches the restriction of access to staff processing orders and the management of unattended sessions, and exposes the central records. The fix is role-based access, automatic session lock, and closing the remote workspace when the central pharmacy is closed.

8Patient records at the central site should be:
Explanation. ASHP: secure access to records at the remote site is required by the central site, while central-site records should be inaccessible from the remote site.
9Which is a safety requirement rather than a convenience?
Explanation. Adequacy of the verification technology determines whether the remote check is safe; it is a requirement of the function.

Explanation. Because telepharmacy regulation varies and evolves, the applicable rules must be reviewed before implementation and regularly after.

Part 4 · evidence

What the literature shows, and how to read it

Underpinning. Telehealth interventions group into teleconsultation, telemonitoring, telecollaboration and telesupport, delivered by telephone, video, applications and monitoring devices. Across reviews, telehealth in pharmacy practice generally improves outcomes compared with usual care, with improvements in surrogate markers, fewer medication errors where supporting technology is used, better adherence in some populations, high satisfaction and wider access, but with mixed results for readmission and operational efficiency. In cancer care, mHealth and web-based interventions were the most effective, chiefly for monitoring and symptom management, and pharmacist-specific evidence is limited. Mixed results are a signal of context dependence. The critical reader asks which intervention, delivered how, improved which outcome, for whom, and against what comparison.

Worked example. A study reports a 30 per cent rise in self-reported adherence against no intervention among young, engaged patients. Self-report is a weak measure, no intervention is a weak comparison, and the population may not transfer to older rural patients. The study shows the tool can be used, not that it improves outcomes in your population.

12Across the review of reviews, the delivery mode most commonly used in teleconsultation and telemonitoring was:
Explanation. Telephone-based care was the most common mode, being accessible and inexpensive; remote monitoring devices were used less than expected, partly because of cost.
13Mixed results across telehealth studies should be read as:
Explanation. Context dependence is the signal in mixed results; the manager asks which intervention worked, how, for whom and against what.
14In the cancer literature, the most frequently analysed outcome was:
Explanation. Satisfaction was most frequently analysed, which the authors note is not the same as a treatment result; symptom management and quality of life followed.
15High patient satisfaction with a service is:
Explanation. Satisfaction is a legitimate outcome but not a substitute for clinical benefit.
Part 5 · equity, governance and evaluation

Realising benefit for the whole population

Underpinning. Services that assume connectivity, devices and digital literacy can widen the inequalities they are meant to reduce, because those least able to use them often have the greatest need; equity is a determinant of net benefit. Governance covers data protection in transit and at rest, access control, consent, staff competence and the management of failure, and is owned by the pharmacy leader. Evaluation judges a service against an explicit framework, each dimension with a measurable indicator: effectiveness and safety, access and equity, adoption and use, patient engagement, sustainability. Adoption is not benefit; launch is an event and uptake is necessary but not sufficient.

Worked example. An adherence application is launched and downloaded by 2,000 patients. Six months later, 300 still use it, most of them young and urban, and blood pressure control in the clinic is unchanged. The launch was adoption, not benefit; the reach was inequitable; and only a framework with a baseline could have shown this early.

16The digital divide matters to a pharmacy leader because:
Explanation. Equity is a determinant of whether the service delivers net benefit across the population, not a peripheral concern.
17Which indicator best shows adoption rather than benefit?
Explanation. Downloads measure adoption; the other options measure effectiveness, equity and safety, which are the benefit.
18A pharmacy leader's role in digital health is best described as:
Explanation. The lectures and readings place the pharmacist as the professional who governs, evaluates and, where possible, generates evidence for digital services.
19Write an evaluation plan in five lines: one measurable indicator for each of the five framework dimensions, for a telemonitoring service for patients with hypertension.
